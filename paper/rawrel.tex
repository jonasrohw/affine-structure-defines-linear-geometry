
\subsubsection*{
Cheng \& Lippl:
Balancing
Relational Generalization and Memorization}

\url{https://arxiv.org/abs/2605.22972}

How exceptions are handled in learning and memory is an
important question. In factual knowledge, there are facts that 
do not fit specific subregularities. For example, father and son have the same
last name in some cultures, but this is not true for pairs
like
(Louis Bonaparte,
Napoleon III) and
(Cassius Marcellus Clay Sr.,
Muhammad Ali). In contrast to violations of transitivity
(where $A>B$, $B>C$ hold, but not $A>C$), such facts that do
not follow subregularities are marked, but are not perceived
as violations (NEED REF) and are not difficult to learn and
process for humans.

In general, transitivity and transitive inference are important for
reasoning, but are distinct from factual knowledge, in
particular from subregularities that are expected to hold
for some facts, but not for others. So
the existence of
facts not
complying with all subregularities is expected rather than a
violation as it is for violations of transitivity.


\subsubsection*{Nichani, Lee, Bietti: ... Factual Recall
  ... Associate Memories}

\url{https://arxiv.org/pdf/2412.06538}

They show that, in contrast to the linear storage capacity
(?) of Hopfield networks, transformers have quadratic
capacity due to the fact that decoding to a fixed discrete
set of output tokens via the unembedding matrix does not
require producing the exact target vector, but only a vector
in the right Voronoi cell.

This is a crucial insight for work on ``linear relations'':
ultimately, what is needed is not a function that computes
the correct object embedding, but rather a function that
computes an embedding that lands in the right Voronoi cell.

Their setup is:

\begin{quote}
We primarily focus on the setting where the embeddings
$\{e_x\}_{x \in [N]}$ and $\{u_y\}_{y \in [M]}$
are drawn i.i.d uniformly from the sphere of radius 1.
\end{quote}

Arguably, this setup does not model a transformer that has
been trained on language. Such a transformer encodes the 
semantic structure of the vocabulary in the embedding space.

One could easily show (I think) that their
uniform-sphere
results do not
hold for a real-world setting.
