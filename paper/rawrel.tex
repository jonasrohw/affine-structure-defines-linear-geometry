
\subsubsection*{Valer \ldots\ Marconato: Empirical
Investigation}

\url{https://arxiv.org/abs/2605.22532}

Two important contributions: a new method for estimating and
diagnosing relational linearity and an extensive empirical
investigation of three main variables affecting relational linearity:
layers, paraphrases and models. As far as I can see their aim
is not to elucidate underlying mechanisms.



\subsubsection*{DeepMind: Fact Finding \ldots\ Reverse
Engineer \ldots}
Their goal: investigate
``how MLP layers implement a lookup table for factual
recall''. They find that retrieval of attributes like sport played correspond
to  linear projections (which map from
entity to attribute).
They seem to believe: ``the internal mechanism is just a
look-up table with no structure to interpret.''
``Our best guess for part of what's going on is what we term
the `hash and lookup' hypothesis, but it's messy, false in
its strongest form and this is only part of the story.''
Confusingly, they seem to think of all forms of factual
recall as memorization -- but affine transformations that
generalize to new facts do not meet some criteria for what a
memorization is (memorization is the oppositive of
generalization). They find that there is a head that
``extracts'' the sport played from the last token position
of the sportsperson's name and writes it to the position
where the sport is generated. The most mechanistic description of how the
sport played is actually produced that I can find is in post
2:
``lookup \ldots\
processes the
'concatenated token representation' after layer 1 of the
residual stream, which in itself poorly represents the
athlete's sport (in a linear manner), and produces a
'feature representation' later on in the residual stream,
where sport can be easily linearly extracted \ldots''. I
would agree with the authors that they ultimately fail in
elucidating the mechanism of factual recall.

Main relevance for this paper: The paper shows that the residual stream 
encodes attributes of entities. This is a key motivation for
our experimental setup.

\subsubsection*{Ravfogel et al.: Geometric Factual Recall Transformers}

\url{https://arxiv.org/abs/2605.12426}

They construct a specific mechanism that implements an
affine transformation in GELU MLPs
(page 21/22).
They show that their experimental results with GELU MLPs
(not RELU MLPs)
are
compatible with their proof, but
do not verify what
mechanism was learned by the network (Section 5.2.2).
Note that we find large differences in the mechanisms
learned by GELU and RELU MLPs for affine transformations.

\subsubsection*{
Cheng \& Lippl:
Balancing
Relational Generalization and Memorization}

\url{https://arxiv.org/abs/2605.22972}

How exceptions are handled in learning and memory is an
important question. In factual knowledge, there are facts that 
do not fit specific subregularities. For example, father and son have the same
last name in some cultures, but this is not true for pairs
like
(Louis Bonaparte,
Napoleon III) and
(Cassius Marcellus Clay Sr.,
Muhammad Ali). In contrast to violations of transitivity
(where $A>B$, $B>C$ hold, but not $A>C$), such facts that do
not follow subregularities are marked, but are not perceived
as violations (NEED REF) and are not difficult to learn and
process for humans.

In general, transitivity and transitive inference are important for
reasoning, but are distinct from factual knowledge, in
particular from subregularities that are expected to hold
for some facts, but not for others. So
the existence of
facts not
complying with all subregularities is expected rather than a
violation as it is for violations of transitivity.

\subsubsection*{Nichani, Lee, Bietti: ... Factual Recall
  ... Associate Memories}

\url{https://arxiv.org/pdf/2412.06538}

They show that, in contrast to the linear storage capacity
(?) of Hopfield networks, transformers have quadratic
capacity due to the fact that decoding to a fixed discrete
set of output tokens via the unembedding matrix does not
require producing the exact target vector, but only a vector
in the right Voronoi cell.

This is a crucial insight for work on ``linear relations'':
ultimately, what is needed is not a function that computes
the correct object embedding, but rather a function that
computes an embedding that lands in the right Voronoi cell.

Their setup is:

\begin{quote}
We primarily focus on the setting where the embeddings
$\{e_x\}_{x \in [N]}$ and $\{u_y\}_{y \in [M]}$
are drawn i.i.d uniformly from the sphere of radius 1.
\end{quote}

Arguably, this setup does not model a transformer that has
been trained on language. Such a transformer encodes the 
semantic structure of the vocabulary in the embedding space.

One could easily show (I think) that their
uniform-sphere
results do not
hold for a real-world setting.
